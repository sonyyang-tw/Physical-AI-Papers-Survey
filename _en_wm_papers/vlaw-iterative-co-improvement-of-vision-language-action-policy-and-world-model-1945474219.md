**Paper** : [VLAW: Iterative Co-Improvement of Vision-Language-Action Policy and World Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12063)  
**Source** : arXiv  
**arXiv ID** : 2602.12063

### Abstract

This paper aims to improve the performance and reliability of vision-language-action (VLA) models through iterative online interaction. Because collecting policy rollouts in the real world is expensive, the authors explore whether a learned simulator—specifically, an action-conditioned video generation model—can be used to produce additional rollout data. However, existing world models lack the physical fidelity required for policy improvement: most are trained on demonstration datasets that lack coverage of diverse physical interactions (especially failure cases), and they struggle to accurately model the subtle but critical physical details of contact-rich object manipulation. The authors propose a simple iterative improvement algorithm that uses real-world rollout data to improve the fidelity of the world model, and then uses the improved world model to generate supplementary synthetic data that in turn improves the VLA model. In real-robot experiments, this method improves the performance of a state-of-the-art VLA model on multiple downstream tasks, achieving a 39.2% absolute success rate improvement over the base policy, with an additional 11.6% gain from training on the generated synthetic rollouts.

  * Problem being addressed: existing world models lack "failure case" coverage and detailed coverage of contact physics in their training data, so their fidelity is insufficient to support policy improvement (i.e., training a policy on synthetic data generated by the world model may backfire or yield ineffective improvements due to simulation distortions).
  * Main method: the authors propose a simple iterative co-improvement algorithm: (1) use real-world policy rollout data (including both successes and failures) to improve the fidelity of the world model (an action-conditioned video generation model); (2) use the fidelity-improved world model to generate supplementary synthetic rollout data; (3) use this synthetic data to train/fine-tune the VLA policy. This process is repeated iteratively, allowing the world model and the VLA policy to co-improve.
  * Difference from prior approaches: unlike methods that simply "train a world model once and then use it to generate data to improve the policy," VLAW emphasizes iteration—the world model itself must be continually corrected with real-world data, with particular emphasis on covering failure cases and the physical details of contact-rich scenes, rather than relying solely on demonstration data from a one-time training pass. This addresses the core problem of "insufficient world model fidelity leading to synthetic data that fails to effectively improve the policy."
  * Description of key method design: the overall pipeline can be understood as a closed loop—the real robot first executes rollouts with the current VLA policy (collecting real interaction data including successes and failures); this real data (especially the failure cases) is used to retrain/fine-tune the world model so it more accurately simulates contact physics and failure modes; the updated world model is then used to generate a large amount of action-conditioned synthetic rollout data (including the visual consequences of the policy taking different actions, rendered in imagination); finally, this synthetic data is used to supervise fine-tuning of the VLA policy, improving its success rate. The whole cycle can be repeated over multiple rounds, with the quality of both the world model and the VLA policy expected to improve round over round.



### Result

  * On multiple downstream real-robot tasks, VLAW achieves a 39.2% absolute success rate improvement over the base policy.
  * Of this, the step of "training on the generated synthetic rollouts" alone contributes an additional 11.6% success rate improvement (the remainder is presumably attributable to iteratively improving the world model itself and other pipeline steps, but the abstract does not break down the specific contribution of each step; this needs to be verified against the full paper).
  * The abstract does not provide a direct numerical comparison with Ctrl-World (an earlier work by the same author group, Yanjiang Guo and Chelsea Finn) under the same tasks/benchmarks. Although VLAW is clearly an extension of the Ctrl-World concept, whether the experimental settings (tasks, base policy models) of the two papers are fully consistent needs to be verified against comparison data from other papers.



### Limitation

  * The abstract explicitly identifies the source of the limitation: the existing (pre-improvement) world model's "training data lacks coverage of diverse physical interactions, particularly failure cases," and it "struggles to accurately model the subtle but critical physical details of contact-rich object manipulation." This is self-reported by the authors and is precisely the core problem VLAW attempts to solve, while also implicitly pointing to the fundamental challenge underlying this class of methods.
  * A potential weakness inferred from the results: although the 39.2% improvement is significant, iterative co-improvement algorithms typically require multiple rounds of "real rollout collection → world model update → synthetic data generation → policy fine-tuning," meaning that some amount of real-world data collection is still required (though more efficient than pure manual annotation/correction). The overall data collection and computation cost, as well as the marginal returns across iteration rounds, are not detailed in the abstract and need to be verified against the full paper.



### Related work

  * This paper is highly related to Ctrl-World (arXiv:2510.10125) and shares some authors (Yanjiang Guo, Lucy Xiaoyang Shi, Jianyu Chen, Chelsea Finn), and can be viewed as a direct extension of the Ctrl-World work—Ctrl-World established the foundational architecture of a controllable multi-view world model, while VLAW further focuses on the problem of "insufficient world model fidelity itself" and proposes an iterative solution.
  * It belongs to the same research direction as WEAVER and τ0-WM—"using world models to assist VLA policy improvement"—and is suitable for comparison against different data strategies (WEAVER/τ0-WM emphasize architectural unification and efficiency, while VLAW emphasizes iterative data fidelity improvement).
  * Worth surveying: high, especially for readers who want to understand the practical issue of "how insufficient world model fidelity affects downstream policy improvement effectiveness."



### Conclusion

  * Overall assessment: worth referencing. VLAW presents an intuitive but important insight—even with a controllable world model like Ctrl-World, if its fidelity (especially regarding failure cases and contact physics details) is insufficient, directly using it to generate synthetic data for policy training has limited effectiveness. Using iterative real-data feedback to continuously correct the world model is a practical and effective solution (the 39.2% improvement is quite compelling evidence).
  * Relationship to other important papers: VLAW is a direct follow-on extension of Ctrl-World (same author group), and it implicitly challenges the assumption that "a one-time-trained world model is sufficient to support policy improvement," emphasizing the necessity of iteration and real-data feedback. Compared with WEAVER and τ0-WM, VLAW focuses more on data-level (rather than model-architecture-level) fidelity issues; the three can be viewed as complementary rather than directly competing research directions.
  * ROCm/AMD relevance: the paper focuses on iterative algorithm design and real-robot validation, and does not mention the hardware platform used for training/inference. Based on available information there is no clear connection to ROCm/AMD. Assessing the computational resource requirements and efficiency of this kind of iterative training (which requires repeatedly training both the world model and the VLA policy) on AMD hardware would require verifying the full paper to confirm whether such infrastructure discussion exists (currently not mentioned).
