**Source** : arXiv (cs.RO / cs.AI)  
**arXiv ID** : 2604.19092

### Abstract

RoboWM-Bench is a benchmark centered on "robotic manipulation," used to evaluate video world models in an embodiment-grounded sense. The authors point out that large-scale video world models are already able to produce increasingly realistic future predictions, raising hopes that generated video could serve as a scalable source of supervision for robot learning. However, for embodied manipulation tasks, visual realism alone is not sufficient — the generated interactions must also be physically consistent and actually executable by a robot agent. Existing benchmarks can evaluate visual quality and physical plausibility, but they have not systematically evaluated whether "the predicted behavior can be converted into an executable action sequence to complete a manipulation task." RoboWM-Bench converts generated human-hand and robot manipulation videos into embodied action sequences and executes and verifies these action sequences within a physically grounded simulation environment. Based on "real-to-sim" scene reconstruction and diverse manipulation tasks, RoboWM-Bench supports standardized, reproducible, and scalable evaluation of physical executability. The authors evaluated SOTA video world models and found that "visual plausibility" and "embodied executability" are not always consistent; the analysis identifies several common factors affecting execution performance, including spatial reasoning, contact prediction, and non-physical geometric distortion, especially in complex, long-horizon interactions.

  * **Problem addressed**: Existing video world model evaluation benchmarks focus only on the visual quality and physical plausibility of generated videos (e.g., whether the video looks real, whether object motion follows intuitive physics), but do not evaluate whether these predicted manipulation behaviors can actually be "executed" by a robot to complete the task — a critical gap when actually applying such models to robot learning/supervision signals.
  * **Main method**: RoboWM-Bench proposes a "manipulation-centered, embodiment-grounded" evaluation approach, with the core pipeline including:
    * Real-to-sim scene reconstruction: reconstructing real-world manipulation scenes into a physics simulation environment.
    * Video-to-action sequence conversion: converting human-hand manipulation videos and robot manipulation videos generated by the world model into embodied action sequences (e.g., arm joint angles or end-effector trajectories).
    * Simulated execution verification: actually "executing" these converted action sequences within a physically grounded simulation environment to verify whether the corresponding manipulation task can be successfully completed.
    * Covering diverse manipulation tasks to measure "physical executability" (rather than just visual quality) in a standardized, reproducible, and scalable manner.
  * **Difference from prior approaches**: Previous benchmarks (e.g., those evaluating only visual realism or physical plausibility, such as PhyGround and WorldBench) remain at the level of "does it look right." RoboWM-Bench goes a step further by requiring "can it actually be done" — converting generated video into executable actions and testing success in a simulator — a key advance from visual evaluation to embodied execution evaluation.
  * **Key design details**: The overall pipeline can be envisioned as a translation pipeline — the world model first generates a video of future interactions based on a manipulation task prompt (which may come from a human-hand demonstration video or a robot manipulation video); then a "video-to-action" module extracts object and hand/gripper motion trajectories from the generated video and maps them into robot-executable action sequences (at the joint or end-effector level); these action sequences are fed into a physics simulation environment reconstructed via real-to-sim, where the simulator determines task success according to physical rules (contact mechanics, collision, friction, etc.); by repeating this process across multiple manipulation tasks and multiple world models, a quantitative "physical executability" score is obtained, which can be compared against a purely visual quality score to observe whether the two are consistent.

### Result

  * **Main results**: Multiple SOTA video world models were evaluated, and it was found that "visual plausibility" and "embodied executability" are not always consistent — that is, some visually realistic generated videos fail to successfully execute the manipulation task when converted to actions and tested in the simulation environment. The analysis identifies several common factors causing execution failure: spatial reasoning errors, inaccurate contact prediction, and non-physical geometric distortion, and these issues become more pronounced in complex, long-horizon interaction tasks.
  * **Contributions**: The main contribution enhances the "completeness" of the evaluation dimensions — adding "physical executability," an evaluation aspect not covered by previous benchmarks, allowing researchers to gain a more fine-grained understanding of exactly where models fail (visual generation vs. executable conversion).
  * **Fairness**: The findings in the abstract (that visual plausibility and executability are not always consistent) are qualitative descriptions, without detailed score rankings for specific models. Since this is a new benchmark (first submitted in April 2026), it is necessary to check whether other papers have cited or re-evaluated different sets of models using RoboWM-Bench to verify the generality of the conclusions.

### Limitation

  * **Limitations stated by the authors**: Based only on the arXiv abstract page, the abstract itself does not detail a limitations section, and verification against the discussion section of the full text is needed.
  * **Weaknesses inferred from the results**:
    * The accuracy of the "video-to-action sequence" conversion module itself may become a bottleneck for the evaluation results — if the conversion module itself has errors, it could misjudge some originally executable actions as inexecutable, or vice versa. The paper does not explain how the reliability of the conversion module itself is verified.
    * The precision of real-to-sim scene reconstruction may also limit the authenticity of the evaluation; there is still a sim-to-real gap between the simulation environment and the real physical world, and this benchmark is fundamentally still verified in a simulator rather than on a real robot.
    * The paper notes that "complex, long-horizon interactions" show more pronounced problems, implying the benchmark may have insufficient discriminative power on simple, short-horizon tasks; this point also needs further verification against the task difficulty distribution in the full text.

### Conclusion

  * **Overall assessment**: RoboWM-Bench fills an important gap in existing benchmarks — "visual realism does not equal executability." Its evaluation pipeline design of real-to-sim scene reconstruction and video-to-action sequence conversion has practical reference value, and is a paper worth prioritizing for researchers who want to apply world models to robot manipulation learning/supervision signal generation.
  * **Relationship to other important papers**: This work forms a complementary relationship with PhyGround, WorldBench, and other benchmarks focused on "visual/physical consistency" — the latter focus on "whether generated videos comply with physical laws," while RoboWM-Bench further focuses on "whether the generated manipulation behavior can actually be executed by a robot to complete the task." The three can be viewed as a progressive relationship in evaluating world models — from "does it look real" to "is it physically correct" to "can it actually be done."
  * **ROCm/AMD gaps**: The paper does not mention the hardware or framework information used for training/evaluation, so no clear connection to ROCm/AMD can be identified, and it would not be appropriate to speculate. If an AMD team is using world models to generate supervision signals in the embodied AI/robotic manipulation direction, the "physical executability" evaluation approach provided by RoboWM-Bench is worth adopting as a reference, though the paper itself does not involve ROCm-related content.
