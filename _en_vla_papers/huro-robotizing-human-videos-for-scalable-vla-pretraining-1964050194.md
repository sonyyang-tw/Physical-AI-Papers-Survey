### Abstract

Large-scale human video datasets are an attractive substitute for real robot data, offering highly diverse scenes and manipulation demonstrations at low cost. However, systematic verification of how to bridge the gap between "human embodiment" and "robot embodiment" has been lacking. This paper proposes HuRo, a "robotization" pipeline that converts heterogeneous human activity videos into robot-aligned observations and action trajectories, while filling in intermediate signals missing across annotation levels in the process. Using this pipeline, the authors build the HuRo dataset, covering five human video sources, approximately 630,000 robotized episodes, and 142 million processed frames. On four real-world manipulation tasks, as robotization pretraining scale increases, overall completion rate rises from 51.5% to 80.3%; under spatial and visual out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, completion rate rises even more dramatically from 34.9% to 72.2%. Ablation experiments further show that visual robotization improves OOD robustness, and that end-to-end pretraining with retargeted actions outperforms transfer approaches that only perform visual conversion. This paper has been accepted at CoRL 2026.

### Method

**Problem addressed:** The performance of VLA policies depends heavily on the scale and diversity of real robot demonstration data, but collecting real robot data is extremely costly (requiring teleoperation hardware, manpower, and time). Human activity videos, while vast in scale and diverse in scenes, lack robot action labels, and there is a significant "embodiment gap" between humans and robots in appearance, kinematics, and end-effector morphology. Existing methods either perform "video robotization" only in narrow, task-matched scenarios, or handle "observation alignment" and "action alignment" separately, making scaling difficult. This paper is the first to systematically examine whether "robotized human videos" can provide effective and scalable supervisory signals for VLA pretraining.

**Main method:** HuRo builds a robotization pipeline that converts heterogeneous human videos into "robot-aligned" observation and action sequences: the pipeline first performs multi-level annotation on human videos (e.g., hand/object trajectories, contact events), then retargets these signals into equivalent action labels in the robot action space, while also robotizing the visual content (e.g., replacing human hands with the visual appearance of robot grippers), and inferring missing intermediate signals where annotations are absent. This ensures the final output episodes are consistent with robot data formats along both the observation and action dimensions. Using this pipeline to process five human video sources, the authors construct the HuRo dataset of approximately 630,000 robotized episodes (142 million frames), and use it for end-to-end VLA pretraining, followed by fine-tuning and evaluation on downstream real robot tasks.

**Differences from prior approaches:** Compared to existing methods that separate "action alignment" from "visual/observation alignment," or that are confined to narrow, task-matched scenarios, HuRo provides a scalable, end-to-end integrated robotization pipeline for both observation and action retargeting, and directly uses it to pretrain the complete VLA policy pipeline (rather than merely producing auxiliary representations or synthetic data). The paper's ablation experiments also provide the first quantitative evidence that visual robotization alone improves OOD robustness, and that "joint end-to-end action + visual robotization pretraining" outperforms "visual-transfer-only" approaches.

### Result

On four real-world manipulation tasks, as the scale of robotized human video pretraining increases, overall task completion rate rises from 51.5% to 80.3%; in out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluations involving spatial layout and visual appearance changes, the completion rate rises even more substantially, from 34.9% to 72.2%, showing that scaled robotized human data can effectively compensate for the generalization bottleneck caused by the scarcity of robots' own data. Ablation studies further decompose the source of these gains: visual robotization alone (without action alignment) improves OOD robustness, while full end-to-end pretraining (action retargeting + visual conversion) achieves a substantially larger performance gain than visual transfer alone, demonstrating that the action signal itself has value that cannot be replaced by purely visual conversion. Overall, the paper's results are internally consistent (showing an increasing scaling benefit trend across different tasks and OOD conditions), and align directionally with findings from other large-scale data scaling studies (such as NVIDIA GEAR's EgoScale/DreamDojo), mutually corroborating the fairness of the conclusion that "large-scale human data can effectively supplement the bottleneck of scarce robot data."

### Limitation

The paper itself does not extensively discuss known limitations, but several weaknesses can be inferred from the method and results: (1) The robotization pipeline relies on multi-level annotation and signal inference, and annotation/inference errors (e.g., incorrect hand pose estimation) may be amplified and propagated into downstream action labels; the paper does not present a systematic analysis of sensitivity to such noise. (2) Evaluation is limited to four real-world manipulation tasks, and the diversity of the five human video sources and robot platforms remains limited relative to robot embodiment itself; external validation across more institutions and more robot morphologies (e.g., humanoid dual-arm, quadruped) is still needed. (3) The quality of "robotized" visual conversion (e.g., the realism of replacing human hands with robot grippers) may degrade with scene complexity, and the paper does not provide error-rate analysis of conversion failure cases.

### Related work

Recent research highly relevant to HuRo includes work from the NVIDIA GEAR team, which similarly pursues the "large-scale human data scaling" route: EgoScale (arXiv:2602.16710, already recorded on this page's 2026-09-10 update) verifies the scaling law between first-person human video scale and downstream robot performance; DreamDojo (arXiv:2602.06949, recorded on the World Model Papers page) tackles the same human data scaling problem from a world model pretraining perspective. HuRo shares with these two papers the core motivation of "using large-scale human video to compensate for scarce robot data," but differs in its focus: HuRo concentrates on design choices within the "robotization pipeline" itself (observation vs. action alignment, end-to-end vs. separate) and directly produces trainable VLA pretraining corpora, rather than focusing on scaling law verification or world model representation learning. In addition, HuRo has been formally accepted at CoRL 2026, indicating that its methodology has passed peer review, making it worth tracking for community adoption once its open-source dataset and code are released.

### Conclusion

HuRo offers a systematic, scalable robotization solution to human videos, addressing the core bottleneck of "scarce robot data" in VLA training, and demonstrates its effectiveness through rigorous scaling experiments and ablation analysis. It is a valuable reference work along the data engineering/data engine route, particularly given its finding that "joint end-to-end action + visual conversion outperforms pure visual transfer," which has guiding significance for subsequent similar research. In terms of relationships with other important works, HuRo, EgoScale, and DreamDojo together form three complementary facets of the "large-scale human data scaling" route (robotization pipeline vs. scaling law verification vs. world model pretraining); together they sketch a comprehensive technical map of current industry efforts to "use human data to break through the robot data bottleneck." As for AMD/ROCm, this type of large-scale video robotization and VLA end-to-end pretraining pipeline demands substantial computational resources (processing and training on 630,000 episodes and 142 million frames). This is an area where the ROCm ecosystem could strengthen its large-scale multimodal data preprocessing (video decoding, pose estimation, action retargeting) and distributed training performance, especially since such pipelines are currently highly dependent on the NVIDIA ecosystem toolchain (e.g., commonly used pose estimation/video processing packages and CUDA acceleration). If ROCm can provide equivalent high-performance data preprocessing and training support, it would help lower the barrier to porting such research onto heterogeneous hardware.
