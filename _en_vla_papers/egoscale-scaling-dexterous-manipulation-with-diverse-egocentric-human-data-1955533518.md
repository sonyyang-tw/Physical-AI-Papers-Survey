### Abstract

Human behavior is one of the most scalable data sources for learning physical intelligence, but how to effectively leverage it to train dexterous manipulation remains unclear. Prior work has demonstrated human-to-robot transfer in constrained settings, but whether large-scale human data can support fine-grained, high-degree-of-freedom dexterous manipulation remains an open question. This paper proposes EgoScale, a human-to-dexterous-manipulation transfer framework built on large-scale egocentric human data. The authors train a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model on over 20,854 hours of action-annotated egocentric human video—more than 20 times larger in scale than prior studies—and find a log-linear scaling law between the amount of human data and validation loss. This validation loss correlates strongly with downstream real-robot performance, confirming that large-scale human data is a predictable source of supervisory signal. Beyond scale itself, the authors propose a simple two-stage transfer pipeline: large-scale human pretraining followed by lightweight, alignment-based human-robot mid-training. This approach equips the model with strong long-horizon dexterous manipulation capability and enables one-shot task adaptation with minimal robot supervision. The final policy achieves a 54% average success rate improvement over a no-pretraining baseline on a 22-degree-of-freedom dexterous hand, and transfers effectively to hands with fewer degrees of freedom, showing that large-scale human motion provides reusable, embodiment-agnostic motion priors.

### Method

**Problem to be solved**: VLA models are highly dependent on robot teleoperation data, but such data is extremely costly to collect and difficult to scale, especially for high-DoF dexterous manipulation such as humanoid hands. The dexterous manipulation behaviors humans perform every day are, in theory, the richest and most scalable data source, but it has previously been unclear whether (1) large-scale egocentric human video can genuinely support fine-grained, high-DoF manipulation learning, and (2) whether such data yields predictable benefits at scale (a scaling law).

**Main method**: EgoScale consists of two stages. The first stage is large-scale human pretraining—training a VLA backbone on 20,854 hours of action-annotated egocentric human video (with pseudo-action labels obtained via hand/body pose estimation), covering far greater scene and skill diversity than robot datasets. The second stage is lightweight, alignment-based human-robot mid-training: using a small amount of target-robot data to align the pretrained backbone to the actual robot embodiment (a 22-DoF dexterous hand), enabling long-horizon dexterous manipulation and one-shot task adaptation.

**Difference from prior approaches**: Prior human-to-robot transfer research has largely been confined to constrained scenes and small-scale data, lacking systematic validation of the benefit of "data scale" itself. EgoScale is the first to systematically quantify, on human data exceeding 20,000 hours (20x prior work), a log-linear scaling law between data scale and validation loss, and to demonstrate that this validation loss reliably predicts downstream real-robot performance—transforming "large-scale human data" from an auxiliary technique into a predictable core supervisory signal source with plannable return on investment. This is a dual contribution of methodology and empirical evidence, not merely a matter of stacking more data.

### Result

**What the results show, and which aspects are primarily enhanced**: The final policy achieves a 54% average success rate improvement over the no-pretraining baseline on a 22-DoF dexterous hand, and this policy transfers effectively to hands with fewer degrees of freedom, showing that the learned motion priors are generalizable across embodiments. Furthermore, there is a clear log-linear relationship between validation loss and human data scale, and this validation loss correlates strongly with downstream real-robot task performance—an instructive quantitative contribution beyond the absolute performance gains.

**Whether the results are fair, and whether other papers' results conflict with this paper's**: The paper is a rigorous academic output from a collaboration between the NVIDIA GEAR team (including Linxi "Jim" Fan, Yuke Zhu, Danfei Xu, and others), UC Berkeley (Trevor Darrell), and the University of Maryland (Furong Huang), with a compelling experimental design (scaling law + downstream task correlation validation). No independent third-party reproduction or challenge to the 54% improvement figure has been found in public literature so far; since the work uses a self-constructed large-scale dataset (not a public benchmark), external verification is difficult, and readers should note that the "54%" improvement is highly dependent on the baseline setup and task selection.

### Limitation

**Known limitations**: The paper focuses on validating the effectiveness of the "scaling + alignment" pathway, but there is no thorough sensitivity analysis of how noise/error in the pseudo-action labels (derived from human pose estimation) affects the fine-motor precision of the downstream policy. In addition, all evaluations are based on tasks and hand platforms set up internally by a single institution (NVIDIA GEAR); generalizability across institutions and hardware platforms remains to be externally validated.

**Weaknesses inferred from the results**: Although the model's transfer to lower-DoF hands is "effective," the paper does not clearly quantify how much this transfer performance gap is relative to the native 22-DoF hand, and there may be a loss of fine motor precision due to reduced degrees of freedom; whether the success-rate improvement on long-horizon tasks continues to scale to longer, more complex task chains also remains unclear.

### Related work

**Newer related work found on arXiv**: DreamDojo (arXiv:2602.06949, also pushed to the World Model Papers page in this batch), published nearly simultaneously by the same team (NVIDIA GEAR), adopts a similar strategy of using large-scale egocentric human video, but focuses on world models (video prediction) rather than directly learning action policies. The two papers complement each other, together forming NVIDIA GEAR's dual engine (one world model, one VLA policy) on the research track of "scaling human data for embodied intelligence."

**Assessment of how worthwhile this related work is to survey**: Highly worthwhile. DreamDojo and EgoScale share overlapping authors (Yuke Zhu, Ruijie Zheng, Linxi Fan, among others) and the same infrastructure and data processing pipeline. It is recommended that future surveys read the two papers together to understand how NVIDIA feeds "human video" into both the world model and policy tracks simultaneously.

### Conclusion

**Overall assessment—worth referencing?**: Worth referencing. EgoScale is the first to systematically validate, at a scale of nearly 21,000 hours, the hypothesis that "human data scaling laws can predict downstream robot performance," providing a quantitative basis for the industry's planning of data-collection investments—a methodological contribution, not merely a single model's performance gain. It is valuable for any team considering human video as a supplementary source of robot training data.

**Relationship diagram with other important papers, and where ROCm/AMD still needs to catch up**: In terms of relationships, EgoScale belongs to the same lineage of "generalist robot foundation models" as π0 / π0.5 (Physical Intelligence) and GR00T N1 (NVIDIA), but EgoScale's unique positioning is "replacing robot teleoperation data with scaled human data," which can be seen as a complementary data strategy to these flagship VLA models. It also forms, together with the same team's DreamDojo (world model track) and the earlier GR00T N1.7 (combined with the Cosmos world model), NVIDIA GEAR's complete data-model stack. ROCm/AMD's gaps in this area are mainly: (1) the lack of an efficient training framework and operator optimizations tailored for large-scale joint video/action data (VLA training pipelines are currently tightly bound to the NVIDIA CUDA ecosystem); (2) the lack of publicly available, scaling-law-validated large-scale human video datasets and corresponding training recipes. If AMD wants to build differentiation in this area, it could consider investing in performance benchmarking and reference implementations for "joint video-action pretraining" on ROCm, lowering the barrier for migrating such large-scale pretraining workloads to the ROCm platform.
